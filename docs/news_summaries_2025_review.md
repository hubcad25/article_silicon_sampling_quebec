# Résumés d'actualité CES 2025 (test) — à valider

Date limite : 2025-03-24 (début du terrain CPS).

## 1 — Canada-US relations and trade

After Donald Trump's re-election as US president in November 2024 and his inauguration on January 20, 2025, he repeatedly said Canada should become the 51st US state and referred to Justin Trudeau as its 'Governor.' On February 1, 2025, Trump signed an order imposing 25% tariffs on most Canadian goods (10% on energy), which took effect February 4 before a one-month pause after Canada announced border-security measures. On March 4, 2025, the tariffs went into effect; Canada responded with retaliatory tariffs on US goods. Trump also announced 25% tariffs on steel and aluminum imports, effective March 12, 2025.

## 11 — Equality, gender and discrimination

Debate over diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) policy intensified in Canada following US President Trump's January 2025 executive order terminating federal DEI programs in the United States, which fueled similar debate about DEI initiatives at Canadian universities and in the federal public service. A 2024 federal study found that racialized people, Indigenous people and other groups continued to disproportionately report discrimination and unfair treatment. A proposed class action on behalf of current and former Black federal civil servants, alleging racial discrimination in career advancement, continued to work through the courts.

*Sources : https://hrinsider.ca/the-dismantling-of-dei-programs-a-racialized-persons-response/?print=pdf*

## 12 — Freedoms and protest

In late April and May 2024, pro-Palestinian protest encampments appeared on university campuses across Canada, including McGill University, the University of Toronto, the University of Ottawa, the University of British Columbia and the University of Alberta, with demonstrators demanding that institutions divest from companies tied to Israel. Quebec Premier Francois Legault and McGill's administration sought police intervention to dismantle the McGill encampment, and courts were asked to order its removal; encampments elsewhere were cleared by police or voluntarily dismantled over the following months.

## 13 — Elections and democracy

The federal Public Inquiry into Foreign Interference, led by Justice Marie-Josee Hogue, released its final report on January 28, 2025. It concluded that while the conduct of some unnamed parliamentarians had been troubling, there was no evidence any MP had conspired with a foreign state against Canada's interests; it documented several instances of suspected interference, including by China, Russia, Pakistan and India, and issued 51 recommendations to strengthen election security, about half of which could be implemented before the next general election. In 2024, the Liberals lost byelections in previously safe seats in Toronto-St. Paul's (June) and LaSalle-Emard-Verdun (September). On March 23, 2025, Carney asked the Governor General to dissolve Parliament, calling a federal election for April 28, 2025.

## 14 — Federal government and parties

Deputy Prime Minister and Finance Minister Chrystia Freeland resigned from cabinet on December 16, 2024. On January 6, 2025, Justin Trudeau announced he would resign as Liberal leader and prime minister once a successor was chosen, and Parliament was prorogued. A Liberal leadership race ran from late January through early March 2025, with candidates including Mark Carney, Chrystia Freeland and Karina Gould. Mark Carney, former governor of the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England, won on the first ballot on March 9, 2025 with 85.88% of points, and was sworn in as Canada's 24th prime minister on March 14, 2025. Pierre Poilievre remained Conservative leader, Jagmeet Singh led the NDP, and Yves-Francois Blanchet led the Bloc Quebecois. On March 23, 2025, Carney asked the Governor General to dissolve Parliament, calling a federal election for April 28, 2025.

## 15 — Crime, drugs and public safety

Record levels of auto theft prompted a federal National Summit on Combatting Auto Theft in Ottawa on February 8, 2024, where federal, provincial, municipal, industry and law-enforcement representatives endorsed a statement of intent to fight the problem; vehicle thefts had reached roughly 70,000 in 2023, with carjackings also rising sharply in some regions. Separately, political support for supervised drug-consumption sites weakened in several provinces through 2024: Ontario moved to close about ten sites by the following March in favour of new 'treatment hubs,' and Alberta prepared to close some of its sites. Health Canada's 'safer supply' funding, provided since 2020 through the Substance Use and Addictions Program, was due to expire on March 31, 2025, with no renewal yet announced, amid claims from some police officials and researchers that the program had worsened the opioid crisis, while other research found consumption sites reduced overdoses.

*Sources : https://thehub.ca/2025/09/10/deepdive-the-battle-over-supervised-consumption-sites-in-canada/*

## 2 — Foreign policy and conflicts

Russia's war in Ukraine continued into its fourth year by early 2025. Canada maintained sanctions against Russia, including new sanctions targeting Russian drones and its 'shadow fleet' of tankers. Donald Trump's return to the US presidency in January 2025 brought a more transactional approach to alliances, including public disputes over continued US support for Ukraine and a tense late-February 2025 Oval Office meeting between Trump and Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. Canada's relationship with the US was further strained by Trump's tariffs and repeated statements that Canada should become the 51st American state. At the NATO summit in July 2024, Canada pledged to reach the alliance's target of 2% of GDP on defence spending by 2032, a commitment the new US administration pressed Canada to accelerate.

*Sources : https://globalnews.ca/video/11522733/canada-targets-russian-drones-shadow-fleet-with-new-sanctions/amp*

## 3a — Inflation and cost of living

Canadian inflation continued easing through 2024, allowing the Bank of Canada to begin cutting its policy rate from its 5% peak starting in June 2024, with further cuts continuing into early 2025. To ease cost-of-living pressure, the federal government implemented a temporary GST/HST holiday on groceries and other items from December 14, 2024 to February 15, 2025. Housing affordability and rental costs remained a central concern, with the Federal Housing Advocate's 2024-25 report citing a shortfall of 4.4 million affordable homes across the country.

*Sources : https://bcnpha.ca/2024-federal-budget-analysis/*

## 4 — Housing

On April 12, 2024, the federal government released 'Canada's Housing Plan,' an action plan aiming to unlock 3.87 million new homes by 2031, including a minimum of 2 million net new homes, with 53 new and ongoing measures to reduce homebuilding costs and speed up municipal approvals. Through 2024, Ottawa listed additional federal lands for housing development and expanded the Apartment Construction Loan Program. The Federal Housing Advocate's 2024-25 report estimated a shortfall of 4.4 million affordable homes nationally, including 3 million for low-income households.

*Sources : https://askross.ca/?p=40323 ; https://thefutureeconomy.ca/blogs/housing-crisis-in-canada/*

## 8 — Immigration and integration

On October 24, 2024, Immigration Minister Marc Miller announced Canada's 2025-2027 Immigration Levels Plan, reducing permanent resident targets from 500,000 to 395,000 in 2025, 380,000 in 2026 and 365,000 in 2027, citing pressure on housing and social infrastructure. For the first time, the plan set targets to reduce temporary residents (international students and foreign workers) to below 5% of Canada's population by the end of 2026, building on a March 2024 announcement to curb the growth of temporary residents. Starting November 1, 2024, new French- or English-language requirements applied to applicants for post-graduation work permits.

*Sources : https://www.canada.ca/en/immigration-refugees-citizenship/news/2024/10/government-of-canada-reduces-immigration.html*

## 9 — Quebec, language and federalism

Quebec Premier Francois Legault's government kept its permanent immigration levels at roughly 50,000 for 2024 and 2025, around 60,000-66,500 admissions once other categories were counted, resisting calls from business groups for higher numbers and citing the need to protect the French language. Quebec also announced new French-language testing requirements for temporary foreign workers seeking to stay more than three years. Tensions continued between Quebec and the federal government, which had been pursuing a target of 500,000 annual newcomers before announcing reductions to national immigration levels in October 2024.

*Sources : https://montreal.ctvnews.ca/legault-says-quebec-can-t-take-in-more-immigrants-after-feds-set-500k-target-by-2025-1.6135606*

## P — Year overview (main issues of the moment)

Donald Trump's re-election in November 2024 and subsequent tariffs and repeated calls for Canada to become the 51st US state dominated the period, with 25% US tariffs on Canadian goods taking effect March 4, 2025 and Canada retaliating. Justin Trudeau announced his resignation as Liberal leader on January 6, 2025, after Chrystia Freeland's December 2024 cabinet resignation; Mark Carney won the Liberal leadership on March 9, 2025 and became prime minister on March 14, scrapping the federal consumer carbon tax as his first act. The Liberals had lost byelections in Toronto-St. Paul's and LaSalle-Emard-Verdun in 2024. Ottawa also announced major cuts to immigration targets in October 2024, and the Hogue inquiry released its final report on foreign interference on January 28, 2025. Carney called a snap federal election on March 23, 2025, for April 28.
