**In brief.** Both branches try to reproduce how 15 Canadian subgroups (5 regions × 3 age groups)
answered 10 CES 2025 questions, using only data from before 2025. Fine-tuning works much better than
prompting (18 vs 30 answers out of 100 in the wrong place). But **a four-year-old poll, CES 2021
reused unchanged, still beats both (15 out of 100)**. Both branches find the same reason: models get
the direction of subgroup differences right but shrink them to about a third of their real size.

\end{encadre}

# 1. The two branches, in plain terms

**The common test.** We take 10 questions from the 2025 Canadian Election Study (CES 2025) and 15
groups of Canadians (5 regions × 3 age groups). For each group and question, we compare how the
model's fake respondents answered with how real Canadians answered (N = 20,180). Nothing from
CES 2025 is shown to the models: it serves only as the answer key.

**The score: total variation (TV).** The share of answers that would have to move to another option
to match reality. TV = 0.18 means 18 answers out of 100 are in the wrong place. Lower is better.

Both branches follow the same three steps. They differ in what happens at each one.

**What one request looks like (fine-tuning branch, shortened).**

```text
You are a respondent to an opinion survey conducted in 2025.
Age: 18 to 24 · Gender: Man · Province: British Columbia · Income: $100,000 and over …
Your answers to other questions in the survey:
- Left–right, 0 to 10 → 9
- In federal politics, you usually think of yourself as a → Conservative
- … (5 more)
Question: How much should the federal government spend on affordable housing?
Options: Spend less / Spend about the same as now / Spend more / Don't know
```

Justin's request is similar, but the person's details are a bulleted list (including party and
2019 vote) and the 10 questions come together.

**Two differences to keep in mind.** The fine-tuned model has more to go on: a person's real answers
to related questions, and a 2024 snapshot instead of 2021. Its better score therefore mixes two
things, the training and the richer description. And with 10 times more fake respondents per group,
its scores carry less random noise.

**Dropped along the way** (tested, not worth keeping):

- *Bigger model* (Qwen 2.4T): worse than the 9B in every condition; it piles onto neutral answers.
- *Shorter descriptions* (demographics only, without party or left–right): party information is the largest
  single gain for prompting.
- *Changing the 7 questions per question* (picking those closest in meaning to the one asked): worse than always using the same 7, at every training size.
- *More training examples* (100,000 instead of 50,000): no better.
- *Stretching the model's group differences* (Justin's IRT correction, ×3): gains 0.01 overall and hurts 4 of 10 questions.
- *Model's national level + CES 2021 group differences*: 0.165, better than the model alone but worse than CES 2021 alone, and it needs a past poll on the same question.
  Even fed the real 2025 national figures, it does worse (0.098) than CES 2021 gaps (0.064).

**What these scores are worth, in real respondents.** A real poll also misses, by chance, and less
so the more people it asks. We simulated real polls of different sizes drawn from CES 2025 and
measured their TV the same way. The model is "worth" the poll size that misses by as much.

| Mean TV by subgroup | Same error as a real poll of… per subgroup | …in total (15 subgroups) |
|---|:---:|:---:|
| 0.178 (fine-tuned model today) | about 14 people | about 200 |
| 0.151 (CES 2021 reused) | about 20 | about 300 |
| **0.10 (target: useful)** | **about 40** | **about 600** |
| **0.08 (target: reliable)** | **about 65** | **about 1,000, a standard poll** |

One difference: a real poll's error is random and shrinks as it grows; a model's error is a bias and
does not shrink with more fake respondents.

# 4. What both branches find

- **Subgroups are flattened.** Hubert measures model subgroup gaps at about 31% of their real size
  (correlation 0.42; CES 2021: 69%, 0.71). Justin finds the same with an item response model: right
  direction on every question, slope at roughly a third of the human one.
- **Most of the error is the national level.** For fine-tuning and CES 2021 alike, the national error
  (0.157, 0.141) is close to the subgroup error. Giving CES 2021 gaps the real 2025 national figure
  drops the error to 0.064, almost the human floor (0.062).
- **On repeated questions, a past poll is the bar, and it is high.** All 10 questions were asked in
  CES 2021. Silicon sampling should add value on questions with no past version.

# 5. Next steps

## A. Change the test: questions never asked before

A client will ask questions that no past poll has asked. Our 10 questions were all in CES 2021,
which is why reusing CES 2021 is such a strong rival here: in real use, it would not exist.

- **Add a second set of questions:** CES 2025 items never asked in earlier surveys, and removed
  from all training data. This is the score that matters for the product.
- **Keep the current set** with CES 2021 as the target to reach (0.151): on repeated questions, a
  model must at least match a past poll.

## B. Make the fine-tuned model know each person better

The models flatten subgroups because they know too little about each person and fall back on the
average. Today they see 7 of the person's answers.

1. **Show 15 to 20 answers instead of 7:** a fixed core of themes, plus 5 or so answers picked for
   their closeness to the question asked. Cost is not a constraint.
2. **Start at 50,000 examples**, then try 100,000 and 150,000 with the best recipe, training each
   size twice to see how much results vary from one training run to the next.

Temperature stays at 1.0. Raising it makes every group answer more at random, which flattens
them further; lowering it pushes everyone toward the most common answer.

## C. A new approach: from a dossier to a distribution

```{=latex}
\begin{encadre}
```
**Today**, the model plays 15,000 fake respondents, one at a time, and we count their answers.
**Instead**, the model would act like an analyst: we hand it a **dossier** on the new question and it
writes down, in one go, **the percentage of each answer in each of the 15 subgroups**.
```{=latex}
\end{encadre}
```

**What the dossier contains** (all from past surveys, nothing about the new question's answers):

- the question's wording and options;
- how Canadians answered **similar past questions**, nationally;
- how **each subgroup differed from the national figure** on those questions (for instance, how far Quebec 18–34 sits from
  the national figure on each spending item);
- simple summaries of the main attitude dimensions (left–right, trust, economy) by subgroup.

**What it returns:** a table of 15 subgroups × the answer options, in percentages.

**Why it is promising.**

- It gives exactly what a client wants, for a question nobody asked yet.
- It uses what we know is stable: subgroups differ in similar ways across related questions.
- One request per question instead of 15,000.
- A published study finds language models **describe** a group's opinion distribution more
  accurately than they **reproduce** it by role-playing respondents one by one (Meister, Guestrin
  and Hashimoto, NAACL 2025, *Benchmarking Distributional Alignment of Large Language Models*).

**How we would train it.** Every question in our 8 surveys becomes one practice exercise: build its
dossier without that question, ask the model for the 15 distributions, compare with the real
answers. That gives thousands of exercises. Two ways to learn from them:

1. **By example** (as today): the model is shown the right table and learns to write it.
2. **By reward**: the model proposes a table, a small program scores it (the closer to reality, the
   higher), and the model learns to raise its score. This targets our real goal directly. Azure
   offers this for some OpenAI models (o4-mini; GPT-5 on invitation), with a scoring program we
   write ourselves, but not for Llama according to its documentation.

Practice and test questions must cover different topics, or the model would see near-copies of the
test questions while practising.

**How we would judge it.** Against the fine-tuned model of section B, and against a plain statistical
version of the same idea with no language model (predict a new question's subgroup gaps from similar
questions; Justin's August "item encoder"). If the language model cannot beat that, it adds nothing.

**First step, no training needed:** build the dossier for our 10 questions as if they were new
(leaving them out of it) and ask an off-the-shelf model. About ten requests.

## D. Already measured, for later

- **A product option:** one real national figure for the new question (a small national poll) plus
  the model's subgroup differences gives **0.082**, worth about 60 real respondents per subgroup.
  The model then only does what it does well: telling how groups differ.
- **For the next note's appendix:** does the model use the survey year? Same person, same question,
  with "2019" swapped for "2025" in the prompt. If the national answer barely moves, the model
  answers for an average of 2019–2024.
